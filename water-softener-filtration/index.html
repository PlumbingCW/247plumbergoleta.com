What a softener does and what it leaves alone

A water softener swaps the calcium and magnesium that cause hardness for sodium or potassium, using a bed of resin beads. When the beads fill, the control valve flushes them with a salt brine and the cycle starts again. Softened water leaves less scale in pipes, water heaters and fixtures.

A softener does not remove chlorine, bacteria, or most chemicals. Those are handled by filters, such as carbon, and by reverse osmosis. That is why a softener and a filter are often installed together and not as substitutes.

Softener plumbing, bypass valves and drain connections follow the California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), including how the discharge line is air-gapped.

Sizing a softener from hardness and household

Size is based on how much hardness the unit has to remove each day. Multiply your hardness in grains per gallon by the gallons your household uses daily. For example, 21 grains per gallon and 300 gallons a day is about 6,300 grains of hardness a day.

A 32,000-grain unit would then regenerate roughly every five days, ignoring the reserve a good system keeps. Common sizes run from 24,000 to 48,000 grains. Oversizing wastes salt and water, and undersizing regenerates too often.

The plumber measures your actual hardness with a test kit, counts people and bathrooms, and checks flow rate.

Signs a softener or conditioner needs repair

A softener is a small machine with moving parts, and most failures are simple. These signs usually mean service is due.

Hard water is back

Scale, spotty glasses and poor lather return when the resin is spent, the valve fails to regenerate or the unit is in bypass.

The brine tank is full of water

A tank that stays full of water points to a blocked brine line, a stuck valve or a drain restriction.

A salt bridge or mush

Salt can form a crust over a gap, or cake at the bottom. The unit then has no brine to regenerate with.

It runs to drain constantly

A water sound at the drain with no cycle points to a worn valve or seal letting water bypass the bed.

Pressure dropped after the unit

Broken-down resin beads can clog the valve and screens.

The display is blank or stuck

A failed timer, a dead power supply or a stuck motor stops regeneration.

Control valves, brine tanks, bypass valves and resin

Most repairs are in one of four parts, and the plumber diagnoses which.

Control valve

The valve runs the regeneration cycle. Worn seals, a failed motor or a bad circuit board can stop it. A replacement valve head is often a better value than rebuilding a very old one.

Brine tank

The tank holds salt and brine. The most common faults are a salt bridge, a clogged brine well and a broken float. Cleaning the tank and replacing the screen often restores it.

Bypass valves

Bypass valves let you route around the softener for repairs. If one leaks or sticks, hard water can reach the house when it should not, or soft water may not flow when it should. They are inexpensive to replace.

Resin and whole-home filters

Resin lasts many years, but chlorine and iron slowly degrade it. Carbon filter media wears out and needs changing on a schedule.

Backflow, drains and the districts to ask

Goleta Water District says a backflow device is often required because irrigation, pools, water softeners or leaks can create back pressure, and devices on approved projects must be tested annually by a state-certified tester. Ask GWD whether yours does.

Rules on where a softener's brine can drain are set by the water utility and the sanitary district, and no rule could be confirmed for this page. Ask your water utility and your sanitary district, which may be Goleta Sanitary District or Goleta West Sanitary District, before you install.

The California C-36 plumbing contractor class covers replacing and maintaining water conditioning equipment, so a licensed plumber can do the work. Permit rules vary, so ask City of Goleta Building and Safety.
