Why a leak under concrete is a different problem

In homes built on a concrete slab, some supply lines run beneath or through the concrete before they rise into the walls. When one of those lines leaks, the water soaks soil and concrete first, then looks for a crack or joint to rise through. By the time you see it, the leak has often run for weeks.

That delay is the reason a slab leak costs more the longer it runs. Water can undermine the soil, lift flooring and feed mold in the subfloor and baseboards.

Signs the leak is under the floor

No single sign proves a slab leak, but two or three together make it likely. Check these before you call.

A warm spot on the floor

A patch of tile or wood that feels warmer than the rest, especially with the heat off, can mean a hot line is leaking under it.

The sound of running water

A faint hiss or trickle in a quiet house with every tap closed suggests water moving under pressure.

The meter keeps turning

With all water off, a moving low-flow indicator means water is escaping somewhere on the system.

A bill that climbed

A gradual rise over a billing period or two with the same household habits points toward a hidden leak.

Damp, lifting or cupped flooring

Wood that buckles, tile that cracks and carpet that smells musty can all mean moisture from below.

Low hot water pressure

A leak on the hot side can pull pressure at the showers while the cold side feels normal.

The same symptoms can come from a leak in a wall or a running toilet, which is why the meter check and a pressure test come before anyone cuts concrete.

Right now: what to do when a slab leak is suspected

You cannot see the break, but you can limit the damage while you wait for the plumber. These steps cost nothing and can save the floor.

Shut the main water valve

Turn the main shutoff clockwise until it stops. If the meter indicator stops turning, the leak is inside your line.

Turn off the water heater

If the warm spot is on a hot line, switch off the heater or set a gas unit to pilot, so it does not heat or run dry.

Clear the floor

Lift rugs, move furniture and open windows to let damp flooring breathe while you wait.

Watch for gas or power risks

If you smell gas, leave the building first and call your gas utility or 911 before calling a plumber. Stay out of standing water near outlets.

How the leak gets located before concrete is cut

Breaking a slab in the wrong place is the costly mistake. Locating the break first limits the opening to a patch.

  1. 1Confirm with the meter and a pressure test. The plumber isolates the house supply and watches a gauge. A falling gauge with fixtures closed proves a leak, and shutting off sections narrows which line.
  2. 2Listen along the floor. A contact microphone picks up the hiss of escaping water through the slab. The loudest point marks the likely spot.
  3. 3Scan for heat. A thermal camera shows warm patches from hot lines. It works best on a leaking hot line and less well on a cold one.
  4. 4Trace the pipe path. An electronic locator follows the line so the opening lands on pipe, not beside it.
  5. 5Use tracer gas if the signal is weak. A nonflammable hydrogen and nitrogen mix is pushed into an emptied line, and a sensor finds the point where it rises through the floor.

Break the slab, reroute the line or repipe

There are three standard repair paths, and the right one depends on the pipe, the number of leaks and how the home is finished. Each has a real trade-off.

A line with repeated slab leaks is usually a candidate for rerouting or repiping, because patching one spot leaves the rest of the aging pipe in place.

Pipes that run in or under a slab

Copper is the most common material for slab lines in older houses. Copper can develop pinhole leaks from corrosion on the outside, from abrasion where the pipe moves against concrete or rock, or from water chemistry on the inside. Goleta Water District reports a customer average of 21 grains per gallon, and very hard water leaves scale that can narrow pipes over decades.

Galvanized steel, found in much older construction, corrodes from the inside out and can clog before it leaks. PEX is flexible, runs in long sections with few joints and is often sleeved where it passes through concrete. Each material fails differently, so the plumber identifies the pipe before choosing the repair.

A cracked drain can also leak under the floor, and a sewer camera inspection shows whether the drain is the culprit.

Deciding what to do next

A single slab leak in a pipe that is otherwise sound is a candidate for a spot repair. A second leak within a few years is a strong sign the line is wearing out, and rerouting or a whole-house repipe begins to make sense. The plumber lays out both so the decision is yours.

If the first search is still open, start with leak detection to find out whether the leak is under the slab or in a wall. If a line has burst rather than seeped, see burst pipe repair. If the damage reached the water heater closet, water heater repair may be part of the visit.
