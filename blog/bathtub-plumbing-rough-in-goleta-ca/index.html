Bathtub Plumbing Rough-In: What Gets Set Before the Walls Close

A bathtub rough-in is the plumbing that goes into the wall and floor before tile, tub and fixtures arrive. It covers the hot and cold supply stubs, the valve, the drain and trap, and the vent that lets the drain breathe.

Most of it is hidden for decades, so mistakes get sealed in. This guide walks through the heights, pipe sizes and checks a Goleta, CA remodel should settle first, and where a licensed plumber should take over.

What rough-in means and why the details matter

Rough-in is the stage between open framing and finished surfaces. A plumber sets the supply pipes, mounts the valve body to solid blocking, installs the drain assembly and trap, and ties the drain into a vent. Everything is then tested while the pipes are still easy to reach.

The stage matters because the tub itself decides several measurements. A different tub model can shift the drain by an inch or two. Pick the tub first, read its rough-in sheet, and only then cut the floor or order the valve trim.

Tub drains are small pipes with big consequences. A standard bathtub waste and overflow runs to a 1.5 inch trap, and that trap connects to a drain line sloped toward the main stack. If the slope is off or the trap is dry, the tub drains slowly and the bathroom smells.

Typical rough-in measurements to confirm

These ranges are common in residential work. The tub and valve maker's own sheet overrides a general number, so treat this as a checklist of what to look up.

The order a plumber works in

The sequence is not random. Each step makes the next one easier to check.

  1. 1Set the tub first when possible. A cast iron or acrylic tub should sit level on a firm base before the drain is joined, so the waste lines up without strain.
  2. 2Mount blocking for the valve and spout. Horizontal framing behind the valve gives screws something solid to grip, so the trim does not rock after tile goes on.
  3. 3Run the supply lines. Hot goes on the left and cold on the right at the valve, with the stubs cut to the depth the valve maker lists.
  4. 4Build the drain and trap. The waste and overflow join to the trap, then to a drain line that slopes toward the stack at a steady grade.
  5. 5Tie the drain into a vent. The vent is connected before the walls close so the whole system can be tested together.
  6. 6Test before cover. The tub is filled and drained to look for leaks, and supply lines are held under pressure and watched.

Only after those checks pass do the walls get closed. A leak found after tile costs far more than the same leak found at this stage.

Venting: why a tub needs air behind the water

A vent lets air into the drain so water leaves smoothly and the trap keeps its seal. Without it, the tub can drain slowly or pull water out of the trap.

Slow, noisy draining

Water that glugs or whooshes as the tub empties often points to a drain starved of air.

A sewer smell in the bathroom

A trap that loses its water seal lets sewer gas rise through the tub drain.

Gurgling in other fixtures

A shared vent that is undersized or blocked can make a sink or toilet complain when the tub drains.

A drain that sounds fine but backs up

The vent may be open while the drain itself is partly blocked, which is a separate fix.

A vent can tie into the existing system or run on its own to the roof, and the choice depends on the layout.

The access panel nobody wants to plan for

A tub valve and a drain trap are the two parts most likely to need service. A panel in the adjoining wall, closet or ceiling below lets a plumber reach them without breaking tile. Many valve makers specify the panel location, so read the instructions before the framing is closed.

In a two story house, the ceiling below the tub is a natural access point. On a slab, the trap sits below the floor, and access may be limited to the valve side. Cutting an opening later is possible, though it adds patching work to a repair that could have been simple.

If the valve itself fails, a cartridge swap through the trim may be enough. That job sits closer to faucet repair than to new construction, and a panel makes it much easier when the cartridge or stops are stuck.

What a homeowner can do and what needs a plumber

Planning and measuring are fair game for anyone. The pipe work is a different matter.

Reasonable for a homeowner

  • Pick the tub and read its rough-in sheet
  • Mark the planned valve and spout heights on the studs
  • Photograph the open wall before it is covered
  • Decide where the access panel will go

Leave to a licensed plumber

  • Cutting or moving drain lines and traps
  • Soldering or crimping supply connections
  • Setting the vent and checking its slope
  • Pressure testing and drain testing before cover

Inspection in general terms

When a permit is required, an inspector usually looks at the rough plumbing before it is covered. The visit typically checks pipe sizes, support, slope, venting and a test of the drain and supply. Passing this inspection is the reason to leave the walls open until the plumber says the work is ready.

The inspector does not design the bathroom, and a pass does not mean the layout suits you. Keep your own photos of the open wall. They show where pipes and blocking sit if you drill or hang something years later.

Ask your plumber about the California Plumbing Code if trap or vent sizes are unclear.

Common rough-in mistakes in tub remodels

The most frequent error is setting the valve before the tub is chosen. A tub with a different drain position or rim height makes the first set of measurements wrong. A second common error is skipping blocking, which lets the valve move and cracks grout lines.

A third is closing the wall before testing. All of these are cheap to fix on an open wall and expensive after tile, whether the house is in Goleta or a Santa Barbara neighborhood with older bathrooms.
