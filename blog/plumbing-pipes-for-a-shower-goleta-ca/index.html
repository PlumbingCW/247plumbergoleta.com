Plumbing Pipes for a Shower: What Is Behind the Wall

A shower is fed by two small supply pipes, one hot and one cold, that meet at a valve. Water leaves through a drain and a trap. Those pieces are usually copper, PEX or CPVC on the supply side, and ABS, PVC or cast iron on the drain side.

Most of this sits behind tile where you cannot see it. Knowing what is back there helps you judge a remodel quote, spot a slow leak early and know when to call a licensed plumber in Goleta, CA.

Shower pipe materials and where each is used

Supply pipes carry pressurized water. Drain pipes carry waste by gravity. They are different systems and use different materials.

PipeUsed forGood to know
CopperHot and cold supplyLong lasting, joined with solder, and common in older walls
PEXHot and cold supplyFlexible plastic tubing that bends around studs with fewer joints
CPVCHot and cold supplyRigid plastic rated for hot water, glued at each joint
ABS or PVCDrain and trapLight plastic, usually 2 inch at a shower drain
Cast ironOlder drain linesHeavy and quiet, sometimes corroded inside in older houses

The valve is the part that wears out first

A shower valve mixes hot and cold water and sends it to the showerhead. Most modern valves are pressure balance or thermostatic, which means a flush elsewhere in the house does not swing the temperature much. Inside is a cartridge that does the mixing, and that cartridge is what usually fails.

Supply pipes join the valve body through threaded or soldered fittings. The valve is mounted to blocking so it does not move when the handle turns. If the valve is loose, the pipes behind it can flex and eventually leak at a joint.

A dripping showerhead or a handle that no longer holds temperature points to the cartridge or stops. That repair is close to faucet repair in scope, and a plumber can often finish it without opening the wall if the trim has been installed with access in mind.

Shower valves, drains and pans are installed to the California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and a remodel that moves them may need a permit.

The shower pan drain is where leaks hide

A shower floor has a waterproof pan under the tile, and the drain passes through it. The pan is usually a liner or membrane that slopes toward a drain of about 2 inches. Water that gets past the grout still has to go somewhere, and it travels along the liner to weep holes at the drain. If the liner is cut or the drain clamp is loose, water leaks into the subfloor.

Leak signs that deserve a call

A slow leak in a shower wall or pan can run for months before it becomes visible. These are the signs to act on.

A stain on the ceiling below

A brown ring under an upstairs shower often means water is passing through the floor.

Loose or cracked tile

Moisture behind tile softens the setting bed and lets pieces shift.

A musty smell with no visible mold

Damp framing holds odor even when the surface looks clean.

A water bill that jumps with no change in use

A leak on the pressurized side adds to the meter reading around the clock.

Soft or spongy floor near the shower

Subfloor that stays wet loses stiffness and may feel bouncy.

Water at the base of the wall after use

A leak at the valve, pan edge or drain can show up at the baseboard.

If you see any of these, stop using the shower and call. A plumber can pressure test the supply and look for the source with leak detection before anyone cuts into tile.

Goleta water and permit notes for shower work

Goleta Water District's 2025 water quality report gives 21 grains per gallon as the customer average hardness. Scale from hard water collects inside valve cartridges and showerheads, so a valve that comes apart without opening the wall is worth the extra cost.

The district also has a 24 hour rule for fixing leaks, and it stayed in effect after the 2019 shortage ended. A supply leak behind a shower wall counts, so do not leave a known leak running for days.

Permit rules vary, so ask City of Goleta Building and Safety before you open the wall. For Eastern Goleta Valley and other unincorporated addresses, ask County Building and Safety instead.

What a good shower rough-in includes

Whether the shower is new or being rebuilt, these steps protect the work behind the tile.

  1. 1Set solid blocking behind the valve. The valve must not move when the handle turns or the trim is tightened.
  2. 2Run supply pipes with few joints. Fewer joints mean fewer places to leak, which is why PEX is popular for long runs.
  3. 3Install the drain and trap to the slope the plumber chooses. A drain line with the right pitch moves waste without leaving standing water.
  4. 4Test the supply under pressure. The plumber holds the lines under pressure and watches for any drop.
  5. 5Test the pan before tile. The pan is filled with water and left standing so a leak shows before it is covered.

Some jobs add a shut-off for the shower so a repair does not need the whole house turned off. Ask for one while the wall is open.

Repair the valve or open the wall

A new shower can be built to last. An older shower often needs a decision between a small fix and a rebuild.

A repair may be enough when

  • The leak is at the showerhead or tub spout
  • The handle drips but the wall is dry
  • The valve has an access panel
  • Pipes are in good shape and joints are dry

Opening the wall makes sense when

  • The ceiling below is stained
  • The valve body is loose or corroded
  • Pipes show green or white crust at joints
  • The pan or drain is leaking into the floor

For new showers or relocations, our plumbing installation service covers the rough-in, the valve and the tests.

Older houses and mixed pipe materials

Older homes often combine materials. A copper supply may have been extended with plastic, or a cast iron drain may connect to a newer ABS shower drain. Mixed joints need the right adapters, or they leak at the transition.

On the hilly properties of Hope Ranch, a shower may sit at the end of a long supply run. If your water pressure at that shower drops when another fixture runs, tell the plumber, because a long run and a small pipe can cause it.

Galvanized steel supply pipe, found in some older houses, corrodes from the inside and narrows over time. It is one reason a shower can have weak flow even with a new valve. A plumber can check the pipe at the shower and tell you whether a repipe is worth discussing.
